**Particle Size and Shape**
==========================

**Introduction**
---------------

Particle size and shape play a crucial role in various engineering applications, including fluid mechanics and mechanical operations. Understanding the concepts related to particle behavior in fluids is essential for designing efficient systems and predicting outcomes.

**Core Concepts**
-----------------

### Stokes' Law

Stokes' law describes the settling velocity of a spherical particle in a fluid under gravity. It states that the terminal velocity (vt) of a particle is directly proportional to its diameter (d), density difference between the particle and the fluid (ρp - ρf), and gravitational acceleration (g). The proportionality constant depends on the viscosity of the fluid (μ).

$ v_t = \frac{d^2 g (\rho_p - \rho_f)}{18 \mu} $

### Terminal Velocity

Terminal velocity is the maximum speed an object reaches as it falls through a fluid. It occurs when the force of gravity pulling the particle downwards equals the drag force opposing its motion.

**Key Formulas/Theorems**
------------------------

$ v_t = \frac{d^2 g (\rho_p - \rho_f)}{18 \mu} $

### Problem Solving Patterns
----------------------------

1.  **Identify the type of problem**: Determine whether Stokes' law applies.
2.  **Plug in values**: Use given values for particle diameter, density difference, and fluid viscosity to calculate terminal velocity.

**Examples with Solutions**
-------------------------

**Example 1**

A spherical particle with a diameter of 100 μm is released at the bottom of a tank filled with water (density = 1000 kg/m³). The particle has a density of 800 kg/m³. Assuming Stokes' law applies, calculate the terminal velocity.

Given:

*   d = 100 μm = 10⁻⁵ m
*   ρp = 800 kg/m³
*   ρf = 1000 kg/m³
*   g = 10 m/s²
*   μ = 3 × 10⁻³ Pa·s

Solution:

$v_t = \frac{(10^{-5})^2 \times 10 \times (800 - 1000)}{18 \times 3 \times 10^{-3}}$
$v_t ≈ 1.11 \times 10^{-6} m/s$

**Common Pitfalls**
------------------

*   **Incorrect units**: Ensure all values have the same units before plugging them into Stokes' law.
*   **Overlooking assumptions**: Check if Stokes' law applies to the problem at hand.
