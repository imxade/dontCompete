# Thermodynamic Properties of Pure Substances: Equation of State and Residual Properties
## Introduction
Thermodynamics is a branch of physics that deals with heat, temperature, and their relation to energy, work, and properties of matter. In this section, we will focus on the thermodynamic properties of pure substances, equation of state, and residual properties. This knowledge is essential for solving process calculations and thermodynamic problems.

## Core Concepts
### Thermodynamic Properties
Thermodynamic properties are those that can be measured or calculated using thermodynamic principles. These include:

* Specific heat capacity (Cp, Cv)
* Entropy (S)
* Enthalpy (H)
* Gibbs free energy (G)
* Helmholtz free energy (A)

### Equation of State
An equation of state is a mathematical relation between the pressure (P), volume (V), and temperature (T) of a substance. It describes how these properties are interrelated.

* Ideal Gas Law: PV = nRT (where R is the gas constant)
* Real Gas Equations:
	+ Van der Waals Equation: (P + a/V^2)(V - b) = RT
	+ Redlich-Kwong Equation: P = RT/(V - b) - a/(T^(1/2)V(V - 2b))

### Residual Properties
Residual properties are those that depend on the departure from ideal behavior. These include:

* Compressibility factor (Z)
* Fugacity coefficient (φ)

## Problem Solving Patterns
### Separation of Mixtures
When separating a mixture into its pure components, we need to consider the minimum work required.

* Use the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) to determine if separation is spontaneous.
* Calculate the entropy change (ΔS) for each component.
* Determine the maximum work that can be extracted from the system.

## Examples with Solutions
### Example 1: Separation of CO2 and Inert Gas
Given a mixture at 1 bar and 300 K consisting of 20 mol % CO2 and 80 mol % inert gas, calculate the minimum work required to separate 100 mol of this mixture into pure CO2 and inert gas.

* Use the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for each component.
* Calculate the entropy change (ΔS) for each component.

## Common Pitfalls
* Not considering residual properties when dealing with real gases.
* Failing to account for changes in entropy and Gibbs free energy during separation processes.
* Incorrectly applying thermodynamic principles to complex systems.
